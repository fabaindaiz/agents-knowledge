For work that changes a system under use, continuous review beats unattended autonomy, and the
method should say so where it describes how a session runs. The reason is not that the agent is
unreliable in general; it is that its mistakes are cheapest in the minutes after they are made and
compound afterwards. A human reviewing as the work lands catches the class of error no check can
see — work put on the wrong branch, a decision taken on a stale measurement, a report that reads
as finished — while the checks catch the class the human would not read line by line. The
corollary is where autonomy is worth asking for: in the **preparation** of work, not in its
execution.

## Evidence

One long session across several repositories of one system, run with the human reviewing and
merging continuously. Every mistake the agent made was caught within minutes, and by the two
mechanisms in alternation: the repository's own checks found an invented record id, a generated
artefact that broke a document rule, a citation to a record defined on another branch, and a
formatting command that rewrote a hundred and forty files; the human found two reports of work as
ready that measured only half of what their own next command would evaluate, and one case of a
session's own closing work committed onto a feature branch, which no check can judge because
nothing in the repository knows which branch the work belonged on. Each of those, unattended,
would have been discovered later by someone with less context, in a state harder to unwind.

The same session shows the other half: the human held the publishing step, so every delivery
waited for them — which is also why the agent's readiness reports mattered enough to be wrong
twice. Supervision removes a class of risk and adds a dependency; the trade was worth it here, and
the human said so in their own words at the close.
